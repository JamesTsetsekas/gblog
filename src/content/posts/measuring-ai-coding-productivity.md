An agent opens a pull request before lunch. The diff looks plausible, the tests pass, and the change is ready for review. The author has moved on. Then a reviewer spends an hour tracing an invented assumption, and the author has to redo the change. How much time did the agent save?

I'd start by measuring the work it takes to get an issue through review, including the time somebody else spends finding and fixing problems. A fast first draft can still be a real benefit. To know what that benefit is worth, I need to see what happened after the draft.

## What METR measured

METR's early-2025 study recruited 16 experienced open-source developers to work on 246 real issues in repositories they already knew. Each issue was randomly assigned to a condition that allowed generative AI or disallowed it. The AI-allowed work primarily used Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 or 3.7 Sonnet. Developers recorded their screens and reported implementation time. The estimated effect of allowing AI was a **19% increase in completion time**, with a reported confidence interval from 2% to 39% longer. Developers had anticipated a 24% reduction and, afterward, estimated a 20% reduction. Those last two numbers describe beliefs. The 19% comes from comparing task times under random assignment in that particular setting. [METR's July 2025 study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), [Study paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)

The February 2026 update reported a second experiment that started in August 2025. It included 57 developers, 143 repositories, and more than 800 tasks. The preliminary point estimates suggested shorter task times with AI, by 18% for the ten returning developers and 4% for newly recruited developers. Both reported intervals included zero. METR judged the second study an unreliable measure of the underlying effect. More developers declined to participate because they'd sometimes have to work without AI, and some volunteers withheld tasks they especially wanted AI for. Lower pay could also have changed who enrolled. Participants running several agents at once had trouble reporting time per task, and some assigned tasks went unfinished. Random assignment can balance the issues entered in a trial. It cannot recover the work that never made it in. [METR's February 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

METR outlined possible redesigns including shorter, more intensive experiments, alongside observational work and questionnaires. Its May report surveyed 349 technical workers. Depending on the question, the median responses put the value of their work at 1.4 to 2 times what they believed they could produce without AI. That was a convenience survey about perceived value, with selection and recall limitations. In November, METR assessed a separate question about acceleration of frontier model research at Anthropic, drawing on internal evidence, interviews, and capability trends. That assessment concerns a different organization and outcome. The evidence in these reports spans timed issue work, beliefs about value, and research progress. Each number needs its own unit and population. [February redesign proposals](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/#other-means-of-measuring-productivity), [May survey](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-11-ai-usage-survey/), [November R&D assessment](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/)

## The pilot I'd run

Suppose a team wants to decide whether to pay for an agent for routine maintenance in one repository. I'd run a short pilot on incoming issues the team already plans to do. The design below is a proposal. I haven't run it or collected the team measurements it describes.

**Choose the work before assigning access.** Agree on eligible issues, such as routine bug fixes and bounded maintenance, and record their expected difficulty and acceptance criteria. List exclusions while the team still has no idea which condition each issue will get. Ask who is willing to participate under either condition and record participation and task coverage in aggregate. A result from volunteers doing small fixes can guide that workflow. It has limited reach if the regular users or most difficult tasks stay outside the sample.

**Randomize comparable issues.** Where feasible, pair similarly scoped issues assigned to the same developer and randomly designate one as AI-allowed. Use different issues so nobody implements a solution they already learned. Both conditions have ordinary documentation, tools, and tests. One also allows a named agent configuration. Give participants time to get familiar with it before the pilot, record version changes, and keep assignments in the analysis even if the developer doesn't use the agent or the issue gets abandoned. A comparison of heavy users with everyone else would bring task choice and developer preference back into the result.

**Stop the clock at acceptance.** Both conditions go through the same CI and review criteria. Reviewers can judge against the issue description and acceptance checks with assignment labels hidden where practical. Reviewers may still infer the condition from a diff. Start the elapsed clock when work begins and stop it when required checks and review pass, or at a fixed cutoff for an unfinished issue. Log team labor separately, including author research, prompting, verification, reviewer time, and rework. If the agent runs for 20 minutes while the author does unrelated work, the 20 minutes can contribute to lead time without adding 20 minutes of labor. Review queue time works the same way. The SPACE framework offers a useful reminder that activity, performance, collaboration, and developer experience measure different parts of the work. [SPACE research paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-space-of-developer-productivity-theres-more-to-it-than-you-think/)

Here is an example of the arithmetic, with made-up numbers measured as active human labor. Suppose an assisted change takes 35 author-minutes to prepare, 40 reviewer-minutes to review, and 20 author-minutes to revise: 95 person-minutes. A conventional change taking 55 author-minutes, 15 reviewer-minutes, and 5 author-minutes at those stages totals 75 person-minutes. The assisted change uses less initial author labor but more total team labor. These inputs do not establish which draft arrives earlier on the clock. An experiment needs multiple comparable issues to estimate whether the labor difference holds.

## Read the result as a team decision

I'd report the fraction of all assigned issues accepted within the same prespecified elapsed-time window, plus total team labor per accepted issue. An unfinished issue remains an unfinished outcome at the cutoff; its cutoff time is never recorded as an acceptance time. Any elapsed-time summary restricted to accepted issues would be labeled descriptive, since the two conditions may complete different subsets. The labor numerator would include time spent on unfinished assignments. I'd show their count and status alongside the ratio so failure costs stay visible. If an arm accepts zero issues, report the labor and completion count and leave the ratio undefined. I'd also record attributable follow-up defects and reverts for the same fourteen days after each merge. A small pilot could find conspicuous cleanup costs. It would have little power to compare rare regressions. A short effort or flow survey can add context to the clocks, especially when developers prefer a workflow that takes similar time.

A team needs to decide in advance how much time saved would justify the expense and which quality outcomes would change that decision. I'd examine the paired assignments, the range of outcomes, and how much any one issue or developer drives the result. An inconclusive pilot can still show where the instrument broke, such as missing review time or repeated exceptions to assignment. Fixing those before a larger run is more informative than promoting a noisy subgroup into a finding.

There is a second question if the tool changes which issues the team can attempt. Faster routine maintenance and a newly feasible migration are different benefits. A fixed-task pilot answers the first question for the tested workflow. I'd document the newly feasible work separately and ask what it displaced, what value it delivered, and how much it cost to maintain. METR's treatment of task substitution helps explain why the two answers can diverge. [METR's task substitution analysis](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-08-task-substitution-and-uplift/)

For this kind of work, with this setup and the same review bar, how much team time goes into an accepted change? I could ask that question again when the tooling or the team's work changes.
